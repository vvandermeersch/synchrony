In (citeus) we showed that summer solstice appears as an optimal time to transition between major physiological growth and reproductive phases. Given a simple trade-off between predicting annual thermal heat (measured as growing degree days) and remaining thermal heat, we found summer solstice was optimal on average for Europe over the Holocene and future predictions, and North America, but with local variation. In their letter, Bogdziewicz et al. (cite) interpret our results as invalidating the potential role of solstice in mast seeding, we suggest they have  mis-interpretted our work. We explicitly considered whether  summer solstice may control the timing of mast seeding, and our results do not invalidate this hypothesis. In (citeus) we do, however, consider the potential role of other drivers and raise questions about how to differentiate between them given strong covariation.

In their letter, Bogdziewicz et al. (cite) argue that summer solstice is a better cue for masting than accumulation of annual thermal heat because it allows for greater synchrony across individuals, whereas we show annual heat accumulation varies spatially. We do not disagree that we show important local variability in heat accumulation. However, we question their conclusions on two grounds. (1) In many masting trees, the strength of synchrony among individuals decays with distance, inconsistent with cuing directly to summer solstice. (2) The strong covariation between summer solstice and balance in annual heat accumulation makes it difficult to reject either as the primary cue; which was the primary message of our paper. It is possible that the European beech exhibits geographically widespread synchrony in masting, consistent with cuing to summer solstice, but this dynamic is not typical of masting trees. Further, showing a masting pattern consistent with cuing to summer solstice is not the same as demonstrating trees cue to summer solstice.

In (citeus) we discuss the challenge in differentiating between cues. Current work on whether the summer solstice starts a ``\emph{temperature sensing window}" has focused on a statistical correlation between an annual metric of seed production and daily temperature, computed over a certain moving-window and found that this timing may occur near the summer solstice on average, but the timing of this correlation varies across space (Journe et al., Fig. 1 in Bogdziewicz). These results echo ours (citeus). We are confused as to where Bogdziewicz et al. see a discrepancy between these findings and ours. Identifying what level of variation invalidates the hypothesis seems an important question, especially as research on the role of the summer solstice in both growth and mast seeding acknowledge that their results suggest the most likely date based on their statistics is before the solstice (e.g., Fig. 1 in submitted response where the `window of opportunity' appears to begin around XX). 

Understanding what scale of variation to expect on average seems equally important as developing testable predictions for variation across space. Based on Bogdziewicz et al. (cite their response) we understand plants across the northern hemisphere would be predicted to mast seed in synchrony, but this is not common (CITES?). Further, while we agree that many economy of scale hypotheses for masting require some level of synchrony to lead to increased fitness, most would not predict such large-scale synchrony (cite Davies and Macpherson) and may thus predict cues other than solstice. Even in the case of extremely widespread synchrony, whether the solstice alone would be an ideal cue for masting is not clear from current work. 

Developing more clear and testable predictions to test the role of the solstice---and potentially other cues---in driving masting (and other processes such as growth) will be important for fundamental science and forecasting. We hope our work, which built from eco-evolutionary and climatological perspectives (citeus), can help develop this and guide thinking about what cues plants should use. We argue that careful approaches and multiple hypotheses may be especially critical here, where bounded systems (e.g., the growing season, citebroken stick) and natural climatological trends may lead to interesting patterns with multiple competing explanations leading to very different predictions (cite decens and new chillwork).

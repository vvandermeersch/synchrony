In (citeus) we showed that summer solstice appears as optimal for plants where temperature contributes to growth and reproduction. Given a simple trade-off between predicting annual thermal heat (measured as growing degree days) and remaining thermal heat, we found summer solstice was optimal on average across Europe over the Holocene and future predictions, and in North America, but with variation across space. In their letter, Bogdziewicz et al. (cite) interpret our results as invalidating a potential role of solstice in mast seeding, though they appear to mis-interpret our work. In addition to the fact that we do not test for a trade-off between growth and frost, the hypothesis that summer solstice may control the timing of mast seeding is explicitly one of the hypotheses our paper considers, and we do not believe our results invalidate it. In (citeus) we do, however, consider the potential role of other drivers and raise questions about how to robustly test these hypotheses---including across space.

Bogdziewicz et al. raise concerns that the spatial variation we document in (citeus) challenges the utility of the summer solstice as a cue for mast seeding, but provide few clues on how to interpret the variation that their work (Fig 1, citethem) similarly shows or what level of spatial variation is expected. Current work on whether the summer solstice starts a ``\emph{temperature sensing window}" has focused on a statistical correlation between an annual metric of seed production and daily temperature, computed over a certain moving-window and found that this timing may occur near the summer solstice on average, but the timing of this correlation varies across space (Journe et al., Fig. 1 in Bogdziewicz). These results echo ours, as we discussed in (citeus). Given that  all these results---and all empirical results in general---include some amount of variation we are confused as to where Bogdziewicz et al. see a discrepancy. Identifying what level of variation invalidates the hypothesis seems an important question, especially as research on the role of the summer solstice in both growth and mast seeding acknowledge that their results suggest the most likely date based on their statistics is before the solstice (e.g., Fig. 1 in submitted response where the `window of opportunity' appears to begin around XX). 

Understanding what scale of variation to expect on average seems equally important as developing testable predictions for variation across space. Based on Bogdziewicz et al. (cite their response) we understand plants across the northern hemisphere would be predicted to mast seed in synchrony, but this is not common (CITES?). Further, while we agree that many economy of scale hypotheses for masting require some level of synchrony to lead to increased fitness, most would not predict such large-scale synchrony (cite Davies and Macpherson) and may thus predict cues other than solstice. Even in the case of extremely widespread synchrony, whether the solstice alone would be an ideal cue for masting is not clear from current work. 

Developing more clear and testable predictions to test the role of the solstice---and potentially other cues---in driving masting (and other processes such as growth) will be important for fundamental science and forecasting. We hope our work, which built from eco-evolutionary and climatological perspectives (citeus), can help develop this and guide thinking about what cues plants should use. We argue that careful approaches and multiple hypotheses may be especially critical here, where bounded systems (e.g., the growing season, citebroken stick) and natural climatological trends may lead to interesting patterns with multiple competing explanations leading to very different predictions (cite decens and new chillwork).
